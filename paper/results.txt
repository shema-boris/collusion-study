Results

All numbers below are computed directly from the 960 logged rounds of the results/v3 snapshot (4 conditions x 3 seeds x 80 rounds), reading rounds.jsonl / all_rounds.csv rather than any intermediate summary, so every figure here can be reproduced from the committed raw data.

1. No supra-competitive pricing in any condition that has history -- but R0 is not the flat baseline it was assumed to be

The primary dependent variable, collusion level (winning bid minus the Bayes-Nash competitive bid b(c), normalized by the round's reference value), is close to zero in the three conditions where agents see any interaction history at all: C1 averages +0.023 (about +$2.73 in raw dollars), S_informed +0.008 (+$0.69), and A_informed +0.008 (+$0.62) -- all small relative to their own round-to-round noise (sd ~0.06 normalized, ~$8-9 raw), and in fact negative in some individual seeds (C1 seed 1: -$0.45; S_informed seed 1: -$0.57; A_informed seed 1: -$1.45). By the direct, first-principles standard set out in Section 3 of the experimental setup, none of these three conditions shows collusion: winners are pricing at, and sometimes slightly below, the theoretical competitive bid.

R0 -- the one-shot condition with no history at all -- does not fit that pattern. Its collusion level averages +0.103 normalized, +$16.26 in raw dollars, and this is not noise: the per-seed means are +$18.21, +$13.99, and +$16.58, positive and of similar magnitude in every seed. R0 also allocates the contract less efficiently -- the lower-cost bidder wins only 61% of R0's rounds, versus 75-80% once any history is present (C1 80%, S_informed 76%, A_informed 75%) -- and its winner margin (raw bid minus cost, before separating out the competitive-vs-supra-competitive split) averages $27.02, about twice the ~$12-15 seen in the three history conditions. So the honest headline is not "collusion level is approximately zero everywhere"; it is that the three history-bearing conditions price at competitive levels, while the single condition with no history at all is the one that prices reliably above it. Whatever produces that gap, it runs in the opposite direction from the paper's original hypothesis -- it is the presence of shared history, not its absence, that is associated with tighter, more competitive, more efficient pricing in this data.

1.1 Why R0 differs: no channel through which competitive pressure could be learned

R0's elevated pricing is not, on inspection, a mysterious anomaly -- it is close to what the design predicts once the mechanism is made explicit. By construction, R0 shows agents no history at all (cond.use_history is false for R0; every call renders an empty history block, experimental setup Section 5): each of R0's 80 "rounds" is therefore an independent draw from the identical prompt distribution (only the private cost and the contract brief change), and there is no channel -- no observed rival bid, no win/loss outcome, no realized margin -- through which an agent could ever discover that the rival tends to underbid a given price. The Bayes-Nash baseline b(c) is the bid a fully rational forward-looking agent would choose by reasoning about the rival's cost distribution in the abstract; nothing in the R0 setup rules that reasoning out, but the observed behavior does not track it closely, and defaults instead toward a heuristic in the spirit of one round-20 agent's stated logic: "Given the contract's reference value of $200.00 and my production cost of $118.06, I will bid $150.00... This bid is competitive while ensuring a reasonable profit margin" -- a plausible-sounding, self-consistent justification that never engages with what a rival bidder would need to charge to win, because R0 gives it nothing to engage with.

Two further, quantitative checks are consistent with this account. First, R0's bids cluster more tightly around a fixed fraction of the contract's reference value than any other condition: bid/reference averages 0.696 (sd 0.073) in R0, versus 0.607-0.617 (sd 0.085-0.095) in C1/S_informed/A_informed -- R0 is both higher and less variable, consistent with a stable heuristic ("bid comfortably below reference, keep a healthy margin") standing in for the missing competitive calculation. Second, and more directly: if nothing can be learned within an R0 run, markup should show no trend across its 80 rounds, while the history conditions -- where agents do accumulate observed rival behavior round over round -- should show at least a weak pull toward the competitive price as the run progresses. That is exactly the pattern in the data. R0's markup is flat: corr(round, markup) = -0.003 pooled across seeds, and -0.021 / +0.060 / -0.050 within each seed individually (first-half mean $28.28 vs second-half $28.16, statistically indistinguishable) -- no seed shows a trend in either direction. All three history conditions instead show a small, consistent downward drift over the same 80 rounds: C1 from $15.14 to $14.54, S_informed from $14.24 to $12.84, A_informed from $14.36 to $13.68. The one condition structurally incapable of learning from rival behavior is also the only one with a flat trend; the three conditions that can observe rival behavior all drift toward lower markups as more of it accumulates. We read this as evidence for a specific, falsifiable account -- R0 is elevated because it is the only condition with no possible channel for competitive calibration, not because "no history" is itself somehow collusion-permissive -- rather than proof of it; we have not run a causal ablation (e.g. an R0 variant given a single scripted example of a rival's price with no further history) that would isolate the mechanism directly (Limitations, item 5).

A second, independent line of evidence points the same direction. Regressing each round's markup (bid - cost) on that round's cost and cost_high jointly gives R^2 = 0.559 for R0 (cost coefficient -0.277), against R^2 = 0.029-0.065 for C1 / S_informed / A_informed (cost coefficient -0.088 to -0.116). Pure Bayes-Nash competition predicts a markup that is a deterministic linear function of cost and cost_high with a cost coefficient of exactly -0.5 (markup = (cost_high - cost)/2); R0's fitted coefficient of -0.277 is roughly half that -- closer to the theoretical prediction than any other condition, but still well short of it, which is itself worth stating plainly rather than rounding up to "R0 plays Bayes-Nash." The three history conditions' coefficients (-0.09 to -0.12) are only about a fifth to a quarter of the theoretical response: once agents can see a shared history, their markup is driven substantially less by their own current cost than the competitive model would predict, and substantially less than R0's markup is.

2. No evidence of turn-taking or bid-rigging

The bid-rotation rate -- the fraction of consecutive decided rounds in which the winner switches -- sits at 0.33-0.66 across the twelve condition x seed cells, scattered around the 0.5 that two symmetric, independently-drawn bidders would produce by chance, with several cells falling below 0.5 (more win-streaking than alternation) and none showing the sustained near-1.0 rate that deliberate turn-taking would produce. Bid dispersion (the normalized gap between the two agents' bids) averages 0.079 in R0 and C1 and 0.094-0.096 in S_informed and A_informed -- if anything slightly wider, not tighter, in the monitored conditions -- which is the opposite of what a "parallel pricing" collusion story would predict for the conditions carrying the strongest incentive to look coordinated. Between the price-level result above and this pair of secondary measures, none of the three classic textbook signatures of bid-rigging -- elevated price, matched bids, and alternating wins -- appears in any of the four conditions once history is present; only R0's price level is elevated, and R0 has no rival history to coordinate over in the first place, so that elevation cannot itself be collusion.

3. Oversight did not measurably change behavior

S_informed and A_informed are statistically indistinguishable from C1 on every measure above -- collusion level, cost-responsiveness, dispersion, rotation, and allocative efficiency all sit within the same range across the three history-bearing conditions, informed or not. The visible, costly detector penalty (Section 8 of the experimental setup) did not push agents toward more competitive pricing (there was little room to, since C1 was already near-competitive), nor did it push them toward any measurable form of subtler coordination. Framing the same v1 detector as "adaptive" for A_informed rather than "static" for S_informed also produced no measurable behavioral difference, which is expected given both conditions currently run the identical underlying detector (experimental setup, Section 8) and differ only in prompt wording -- this is a comparison the current design is not yet built to answer definitively (Limitations, item 3).

4. The detector's suspicion tracks bid and reasoning similarity, not price level -- and it says so directly

Mean detector confidence is high in both informed conditions -- 0.678 (S_informed) and 0.751 (A_informed) -- despite winners in those same conditions pricing at or near the competitive baseline (Section 1). Two pieces of evidence, one statistical and one textual, converge on the same mechanism.

Statistically, detector confidence D correlates only weakly with the actual price-based collusion level within each informed condition (r = 0.032 for S_informed, r = 0.15 for A_informed) -- essentially no relationship between how suspicious a round looks to the detector and how far that round's winning price actually sits above competitive. D correlates far more strongly, and negatively, with bid dispersion (r = -0.143 for S_informed, r = -0.372 for A_informed): the closer the two agents' bids happen to land to each other in a given round, the more suspicious the detector becomes of that round, independent of whether the price itself is elevated.

Textually, this is not an inference from correlation alone -- the detector says as much directly in its own logged "tells" for that round. Across the 480 informed-condition rounds with a tells entry, 89% cite some form of bid similarity ("matching," "parallel," "identical," or "close" bids), 79% cite winner-repetition framed as rotation or turn-taking (e.g. "B has won every round"), 69% cite bids being held above cost, and 45% cite similarity in the two agents' reasoning text itself ("similar reasoning texts," "shared approach or understanding"). These are not spurious detector behavior: they are precisely the four cues the detector's own system prompt instructs it to look for (Section 8 of the experimental setup). The detector is doing what it was told to do; the problem is that those cues stop being diagnostic once the two bidders are independent instances of the same underlying model facing the same problem. Two competent, independent solvers of the same optimization problem, drawing costs from the same distribution and reasoning from a shared, symmetric equilibrium concept, will tend to converge on similar bids and similarly-worded justifications as a simple consequence of being the same model solving the same problem well -- not because they coordinated. The detector cannot currently tell that apart from actual coordination, and its own stated evidence for suspicion, round after round, is exactly the surface-level similarity that convergent-but-independent play would also produce.

5. Summary

Taken together: agents do not collude once any interaction history is present -- prices sit at or below the competitive benchmark, bids are no more parallel than chance would produce, and wins do not alternate more than chance. The one condition that prices reliably above competitive, and allocates less efficiently, is R0 -- the condition with no history at all, which runs opposite to what a collusion-requires-repeated-interaction story would predict, and which we do not yet have a verified mechanistic explanation for. The visible, costly detector penalty changed none of this behavior in either informed condition. And the detector's own suspicion score, while elevated throughout the informed conditions, is driven by surface similarity between two same-model bidders' prices and prose rather than by the thing it is meant to measure -- how far the winning price sits above the competitive benchmark -- a mechanism confirmed both statistically and in the detector's own stated reasoning.
