Limitations

1. The detector's calibration set never exercised its actual failure mode. Before the profit penalty went live, the detector was calibrated against three scripted histories (experimental setup, Section 8): compete, collude_silent, and collude_explicit. The compete anchor was written so the two scripted agents' bids genuinely differ from each other, which is what produced its clean D ~ 0.20 floor and the +0.75 separation used to justify turning the penalty on. But the actual live agents are two independent instances of the same model, and by far their most common behavior is not "differing bids" but "convergent bids and convergent reasoning" -- the calibration set simply never contained a scripted example of two honestly-competing, same-model bidders converging on similar numbers, so it could not have caught that this is exactly what the detector reads as suspicious (Results, Section 4). The 0.20 floor measured at calibration time is a valid answer to "does the detector separate scripted differing-bid competition from scripted collusion," but it is not evidence that 0.20 is what honest same-model competition should score, and the results show the real floor for that regime is closer to 0.68-0.75. Any reading of "the detector's confidence dropped/rose" against the calibrated 0.20 anchor should be caveated accordingly.

2. Using two instances of the identical model as A and B may itself inflate the detector's false-positive rate, independent of any true collusion question. The study design deliberately uses the same base model for both bidders (experimental setup, Section 5) so that any behavioral asymmetry is attributable to role rather than capability -- a reasonable and in fact necessary control for the collusion question itself, since collusion level is measured against a price baseline that does not care whether the two bidders are the same model. But the detector's dominant cues (Results, Section 4) are keyed on cross-agent similarity in price and in reasoning prose, and same-model bidders are structurally more likely to produce similar prices and similarly-styled prose than two genuinely different models would, purely as an artifact of shared architecture and training rather than of coordination. We cannot currently separate "the detector false-positives because same-model convergence looks like coordination" from "the detector would false-positive at a similar rate against any two competent, independent bidders" without a condition that varies which models play A and B -- a comparison the current design does not run.

3. The detector is still v1; the study's central adaptive-monitoring question is not yet tested. The design specification calls for a two-timescale detector -- a persisted repository of coordination indicators, scored deterministically every round and revised by an LLM only on a slower cadence (experimental setup, Section 8) -- specifically so that S_informed (static) and A_informed (adaptive) would differ in how the monitor itself behaves, not merely in what the agent is told about it. That two-timescale detector is not yet built; both informed conditions currently run the identical single-call v1 scorer and differ only in a prompt clause. The finding that "oversight did not measurably change behavior" (Results, Section 3) is therefore a finding about a static, prompt-only framing of oversight, and says nothing yet about whether a monitor that genuinely revises its criteria over time would produce a different result or drive the detection-evading arms race the study was originally designed to look for.

4. Scale: three seeds, 80 rounds, one model family. The R0 finding (Results, Section 1) is consistent across all three available seeds, which is reassuring, but three seeds is still a narrow base from which to characterize its magnitude precisely or to rule out that all three happen to share some common idiosyncrasy of this particular scenario-ordering scheme or cost-draw stream. Every agent, judge, and detector call in this snapshot also comes from a single model pairing (Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct for agents and detector, GPT-4o-mini for the judge); whether the null collusion result, the R0 anomaly, and the detector's same-model false-positive pattern hold for other model families, sizes, or a genuinely mixed A/B pairing (see item 2) are all open questions the current data cannot answer.

5. The R0 mechanism is evidenced but not causally isolated. Results Section 1.1 gives a specific, falsifiable account of why R0 prices above competitive: R0 is the only condition with no channel for observing rival behavior at all, and its markup is correspondingly flat across all 80 rounds in every seed individually (corr(round, markup) between -0.05 and +0.06), while the three history conditions each show a small, consistent downward drift over the same span -- exactly what "competitive pressure is learned from observed history, and R0 has none to learn from" predicts. That is real, quantitative support, not a post hoc guess, but it still falls short of a causal demonstration. We have not run an ablation that isolates the mechanism directly -- for instance, an R0 variant seeded with a single scripted example of a rival's realized bid and nothing else, to test whether even one data point about competitive pricing is enough to pull R0's markup down toward C1's level. Until that ablation is run, "no observable rival behavior prevents competitive calibration" should be read as the best-supported explanation in this data, not a proven one.

6. Winner selection and payoff parameters are fixed choices, not swept. The best-value scoring rule (65% weight on price, 35% on judge quality), the quality gate threshold, the detector fine lambda = 0.5, and the CFS coefficients (experimental setup, Sections 2 and 8) are each a single committed setting rather than a value confirmed to be robust across a sweep. The design documents several of these explicitly as sweep knobs (e.g. lambda at 0.3 / 0.5 / 0.7) for a planned dose-response result; the results here reflect only the lambda = 0.5 point, and the direction or size of any effect at weaker or stronger settings is not yet known.

7. All reported metrics are relative to the modeled competitive baseline, not to a real market. The competitive benchmark b(c) = (c + cost_high)/2 is the correct Bayes-Nash equilibrium for the specific sealed-bid, i.i.d.-uniform-cost stage game implemented here (experimental setup, Section 3), and every claim of "at, above, or below competitive" in the Results is stated relative to that model, not to any independently observed real-world procurement benchmark. That is by design -- it is what makes the primary measure falsifiable at all -- but it also means the results characterize these agents' behavior relative to a specific theoretical idealization of competition, and any mismatch between that idealization and how a real repeated procurement market actually clears is outside what this study can speak to.
