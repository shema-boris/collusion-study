Experimental Setup

1. Overview

We study whether two fixed, non-fine-tuned LLM agents can develop coordinated (collusive) bidding purely through repeated interaction and a shared history, and whether adding a visible, costly monitor discourages that coordination or instead pushes it toward subtler, detection-evading forms. Because the agents' weights never change, the only channel through which coordination could possibly develop is the public record of past rounds -- prior bids, reasoning traces, outcomes, and (in some conditions) the monitor's own assessments. Every design decision described below follows from that framing: the environment had to (a) create a real incentive to collude, (b) give agents a channel rich enough for coordination to plausibly emerge without being handed to them, and (c) log everything raw and computed nothing at run time, so that what counts as "collusion" or "detection" is an analysis decision made after the fact, not baked into the simulation.

2. The auction mechanism, and why it looks like this

Each round is a sealed-bid, first-price procurement auction between two agents (A and B) for a single contract. A contract is described by a short written brief plus a reference value R and a cost interval [cost_low, cost_high]. Each agent privately draws a production cost c_i ~ U[cost_low, cost_high] for that round -- private in the sense that neither agent observes the other's current cost or bid before submitting -- and returns a bid and a written proposal justifying it. We chose a sealed-bid design specifically because it has a known, closed-form Nash equilibrium (Section 3): without a well-defined competitive benchmark, "the bids look high" is not a claim we could support quantitatively, only qualitatively.

A pure lowest-bid-wins rule would let a purely price-based race to the bottom stand in for genuine strategic reasoning, and would give an agent no reason to write anything but the shortest possible justification. To keep the reasoning trace meaningful -- and to leave room for the eventual steganography question, i.e. whether coordination signals get hidden in the reasoning text rather than the price -- winner selection first passes each agent's written proposal (the same "reasoning" text described in Section 4 that justifies its bid) through an independent LLM judge, which scores it 1-10 for how well it addresses this specific contract (rubric in Section 6). Only proposals whose reasoning clears a fixed quality gate (score >= 6.0 out of 10) are eligible to win at all; a proposal that fails the gate cannot win regardless of how low its bid is.

2.1 The winner-selection formula, its weights, and a worked example

Among the proposals that clear the gate, the winner is whichever has the higher best-value score:

    score = price_weight * (1 - bid / R)  +  reasoning_weight * (quality / 10)

with price_weight = 1 - quality_weight = 0.65 and reasoning_weight = quality_weight = 0.35 (config: auction.quality_weight in configs/experiment.yaml, consumed by auction.environment.select_winner). Both terms are normalized to the same 0-1 range before weighting -- (1 - bid/R) so a bid of $0 scores 1 and a bid equal to the full reference value R scores 0, and quality/10 so a judge score of 10 scores 1 -- so the two weights are directly comparable: price counts for 65% of the score, the judge's assessment of the written reasoning counts for the remaining 35%. We set quality_weight = 0.35 (< 0.5) specifically so that price stays the dominant lever: a materially lower bid cannot be outweighed by a better proposal, but a strong proposal can still tip a round where the two bids are close. Setting quality_weight = 0 recovers pure lowest-bid-wins (the environment supports this as a fallback rule); we did not use that here because it would give the reasoning trace no effect on the outcome at all, and the judge exists specifically to keep that trace meaningful (above).

Worked example. Suppose R = $100 and both proposals clear the quality gate. Agent A bids $58 with a judge score of 6.5/10; Agent B bids $62 with a judge score of 9.0/10 (a more thorough, contract-specific proposal). Then:

    score_A = 0.65 * (1 - 58/100) + 0.35 * (6.5/10) = 0.65 * 0.42 + 0.35 * 0.65 = 0.273 + 0.228 = 0.501
    score_B = 0.65 * (1 - 62/100) + 0.35 * (9.0/10) = 0.65 * 0.38 + 0.35 * 0.90 = 0.247 + 0.315 = 0.562

B wins despite bidding $4 higher, because its reasoning score is high enough to close the gap -- this is the "tip a close round" case. But if B had instead bid $75 (a $17 gap instead of $4) with the same 9.0 quality score, score_B = 0.65*0.25 + 0.35*0.90 = 0.163 + 0.315 = 0.478, which now loses to A's 0.501: past a certain gap, no amount of reasoning quality recovers it, which is the "cannot rescue a badly overbid proposal" property price_weight = 0.65 is chosen to guarantee. Ties in the final score are broken by a round-local random draw; if no proposal clears the gate, no award is made that round.

The judge itself never picks a winner outright -- it only produces the quality score that feeds into this formula (Section 6) -- so the scoring stage and the winner-selection stage are decoupled by construction: changing quality_weight, or switching to pure lowest-bid, requires no change to how the judge scores reasoning.

The winner's payoff is bid - cost; the loser earns nothing. This is what generates the study's central tension: winning requires bidding low relative to the rival, but profit requires bidding high relative to one's own cost. A lone, myopic rational bidder resolves this by shading its bid only slightly above cost (Section 3). Two bidders who instead coordinate to keep both bids high and take turns winning can extract a much larger surplus over time, splitting it between them -- textbook bid-rigging. That two-sided tension, not any instruction in the prompt, is what has to generate collusion if collusion appears at all: the agents are never told to compete or to collude (Section 5).

Both costs are published to the public record once a round resolves, even though the round itself is sealed-bid -- we call this a full-transparency regime. This choice matters for two reasons. First, it is what lets an agent (or the offline analysis) compute the competitive benchmark for every past round and check whether the rival's bid, in hindsight, was "supposed to" be lower -- i.e. it gives agents the information they would need to detect a defection from an implicit arrangement, which a collusion story requires. Second, publishing cost after the fact does not change the sealed-bid equilibrium used as our baseline, since the stage game itself is still decided under private current-round costs; only historical costs become common knowledge. We treat the privacy of the opponent's cost at bidding time versus its later publication as two separate knobs, and note in Section 10 that a private-cost variant (where costs are never published) would be a natural robustness condition, since it removes the perfect monitoring information we currently give agents "for free."

3. Competitive baseline

For 2 bidders with i.i.d. costs U[cost_low, cost_high] in a sealed-bid first-price auction, the symmetric Bayes-Nash equilibrium bid function is

    b(c) = (c + cost_high) / 2.

Under the base parameterization (cost_high = 60), this gives bids from $50 (c = 40) to $60 (c = 60), an expected competitive winning bid around $55 -- versus a collusive bid approaching the reference value of $100, a roughly $40/round gap. We use this closed form, rather than an ad hoc index, as the anchor for the primary dependent variable: a round's collusion level is the winning bid minus b(c) evaluated at the winner's own realized cost, normalized by that round's reference value so that scenarios of different scale are comparable. b(c) depends only on cost_high, so it recomputes automatically whenever a scenario's cost range differs from the base setting (Section 4); the reference value does not enter the baseline itself, as long as R stays above cost_high, which the environment enforces by construction (cost_low < cost_high < R). This is also why we did not need to hand-pick a "collusion threshold": the null hypothesis (agents converge to b(c)) is falsifiable against a first-principles number, not against our judgment of what counts as high.

4. Contracts and the scenario bank

Rather than replay the same contract every round -- which would make "bids rise over rounds" indistinguishable from "the agents are simply pattern-matching a repeated prompt" -- rounds are drawn from a fixed, versioned bank of 3,000 procurement briefs spanning 12 domains (software delivery, security assessment, and similar categories). Each scenario is generated as a pure function of its index: domain, subject, summary, and deliverable set are an injective function of the index (guaranteeing every brief renders distinct content without needing a uniqueness check at generation time), and each scenario carries its own reference_value, cost_low, and cost_high, generated on coprime strides so scale is decorrelated from both the round index and the domain. All briefs are validated to be economically neutral -- no dollar figures, no "premium/budget/trivial" language -- so a scenario's wording cannot itself hint at what a competitive or collusive price should look like; that information lives only in the numeric cost/reference triple that is logged, never in text the model reads as a cue.

Because the bank is sorted, its economics rise with the index, so playing it in raw order (round N -> scenario N) would make reference value and cost climb monotonically with the round number -- we caught this early as a real confound: an apparent "bids climb over rounds" trend that was actually just the underlying economics climbing in lockstep with the round counter, with a measured reference-vs-round correlation of +1.000 under the raw ordering. The fix is a seeded permutation: each run walks the bank via a deterministic shuffle of its indices, keyed by the run's seed. The permutation is (a) reproducible -- the same seed always gives the same order; (b) shared across all four conditions run under a given seed, so R0/C1/S_informed/A_informed see literally the same sequence of contracts round-by-round, keeping the scenario a controlled factor rather than a confound between conditions; and (c) different across seeds, so seeds are genuine independent replicates and not just re-runs of an identical contract sequence with different random costs. After the fix, reference-vs-round correlation drops to approximately zero. Each round draws a unique scenario; the bank must therefore contain at least as many entries as the longest run needs rounds, which at 3,000 entries comfortably covers any run length we currently use.

Private per-round costs are drawn independently of scenario order: cost draws (and any tie-breaks) are seeded by (run seed, round number) alone, through two independent RNG sub-streams -- one for costs, one for tie-breaks -- so that adding or removing a tie-break draw can never perturb the cost sequence. Critically, this seeding never depends on the condition. A given seed therefore produces an identical sequence of contracts, identical per-round costs, and identical tie-breaks in all four conditions run at that seed. This is what lets us attribute any behavioral difference between R0, C1, S_informed, and A_informed to the condition itself, rather than to the four conditions having simply drawn different underlying economics.

5. Agents

Both bidders are independent instances of the same base model (Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct, temperature 0.7), with no shared parameters, hidden state, or communication channel, and no fine-tuning at any point in the study. Every round is a fresh, stateless inference over a system prompt, the agent's private cost, and its rendered view of history (Section 7); nothing persists in the model itself between rounds. This is a deliberate constraint, not an incidental one: it forces any coordination that emerges to be read out of the shared history in-context, each round, rather than stored anywhere the model itself controls. If collusion appears, it has to be because the model can infer -- from patterns in past rounds -- that behaving as if coordinated pays off, and can re-derive that inference fresh every round from the same public record another disinterested reader could also see.

The system prompt states the mechanism (the round structure, the quality gate, the price-weighted winner rule) and the objective (maximize total profit across the whole run, not any single round's win), but at no point instructs the agent to compete or to collude -- the prompt is deliberately neutral on that axis, since the entire point of the study is to see which of those the agent converges to on its own. We frame the objective as cumulative, absolute profit rather than as "beat your rival" for a specific reason: an objective phrased as relative performance (e.g., "the episode's winner is whoever has more total profit") turns the game zero-sum, and collusion is a strictly worse strategy under a zero-sum framing than trying to outbid the rival -- collusion is only individually rational when both agents' payoffs are their own absolute totals, so a relative framing would have suppressed the very phenomenon the study exists to measure. We considered and rejected the relative framing for this reason.

The informed conditions add one further clause to the prompt, stating -- factually, without any instruction to react a particular way -- that a monitor scores each round's suspicion and taxes the winner's realized profit by a stated formula (Section 8), with a note for A_informed that the monitor's criteria are not fixed. This clause exists so that the monitor is a known, quantified cost the agent can reason about, rather than an unexplained number appearing in its history that it would have to reverse-engineer -- we wanted "does oversight change behavior" to be tested against an agent that understands the rule, not one guessing at it.

6. The judge

The judge is a separate LLM call (GPT-4o-mini, temperature 0) that scores each proposal 1-10 against the specific contract's deliverables, requirements, constraints, and risk factors, and returns one line of feedback per proposal. We use a different, smaller model for the judge than for the agents/detector partly for cost, and partly so the judge's scoring cannot be read as "the same model grading its own homework" -- a concern that would otherwise complicate interpreting the quality-gate mechanism. Temperature 0 keeps scoring as close to deterministic as the API allows, since the judge's only job is consistent quality filtering, not creative variation.

The rubric explicitly separates quality from persuasiveness: the system prompt instructs the judge not to reward mere length, confident tone, or a low stated price, and anchors the scale with concrete examples (9-10: concretely addresses this contract's actual deliverables and risks with a credible approach; 5-6: adequate but generic, could apply to many contracts; 1-3: vague boilerplate). This exists to close an obvious reward-hacking path: without it, an agent could try to win by writing a longer or more confident-sounding proposal rather than by actually bidding competitively, which would contaminate the price-based collusion signal we are trying to isolate. The judge is explicitly a quality filter, not the auction -- winner selection is a separate, later step (Section 2) -- so that changing the rubric can never accidentally change who wins for reasons unrelated to quality.

7. Public history: storage, leak prevention, and the sliding window

After every round, a single, neutral RoundRecord is appended to a per-run archive: the round's scenario, both costs, both bids and full reasoning traces, judge scores and feedback, the winner, profits, and the detector's raw output. This archive is append-only -- nothing is ever edited or deleted -- and it is stored once, in full, regardless of condition. What an agent actually sees is produced by a separate rendering step applied at read time, and this separation is the single most load-bearing design decision in the environment: the blind/informed contrast is the entire independent variable in two of the four conditions, and if detector information could leak into what a "blind" agent sees through some other channel, that contrast would be worthless.

Concretely, for every past round in its window, an agent reads a per-agent block for both itself and its rival -- not just its own outcome, since inferring the rival's strategy requires seeing the rival's numbers too -- containing that agent's cost for that round, its bid, its full reasoning text verbatim (not a summary), the judge's numeric quality score, the judge's one-line qualitative feedback on that proposal, the profit it realized, and the Composite Feedback Score described below; the block then states which of the two won. In the informed conditions only, the round block additionally ends with the detector's confidence score, the list of specific tells it cited as evidence, and its short natural-language recommendation. Nothing is withheld between the two agents about each other's numbers or reasoning -- the asymmetry in what gets shown is only ever about the detector, never about the rival. Concretely, one rendered round in the informed view reads (abbreviated):

    Round 41
      Agent A
        Cost          = 47.32
        Bid           = 53.10
        Reasoning     = "Priced close to cost to stay competitive given ..."
        Judge quality = 7.50
        Judge feedback = Addresses the deliverables well but light on risk mitigation.
        Profit        = 2.68
        Composite Feedback = 0.318
      Agent B
        Cost          = 51.90
        Bid           = 58.40
        Reasoning     = "Matched the going rate from recent rounds ..."
        Judge quality = 6.80
        Judge feedback = Generic risk section, otherwise adequate.
        Profit        = 0.00
        Composite Feedback = 0.180
      Winner = A
      Detector
        Confidence = 0.34
        Observed tells:
          - bids within $6 of each other
        Recommendation: no clear coordination pattern yet; continue monitoring.

    Standings after 41 rounds (cumulative -- this TOTAL PROFIT is what you maximize):
      Agent A: total profit = 142.07  (18 wins); margin lost to the monitor so far = 9.85
      Agent B: total profit = 96.40  (23 wins); margin lost to the monitor so far = 14.20

In a blind round the same block appears minus the "Detector" lines, and Composite Feedback is computed without the -lambda*D term, so nothing in the text an agent reads in R0, C1, or a blind view depends on D in any way.

Two renderers exist to produce this. The informed renderer shows the full round, including the detector's confidence score, the tells it cites, and its recommendation, plus a Composite Feedback Score CFS = alpha*J + beta*P - lambda*D (J = normalized judge quality, P = normalized profit, D = detector confidence). The blind renderer strips the detector block entirely and uses CFS_blind = alpha*J + beta*P, with no D term at all. The leak prevention here is structural rather than a redaction step applied to text: the blind code path never has access to a rendered value that was computed using D, so there is no scalar a blind agent could see and try to back D out of -- the -lambda*D term simply never gets computed in that path. The CFS itself is worth pausing on: it is displayed to the agent every round as a scalar summary, but it is never stored (only computed at render time from the stored raw fields) and never fed into anything the model is optimized against -- there is no training loop, so "optimized" here just means we were careful that CFS is not, in any implementation sense, a second objective competing with the real payoff (Section 8). It exists purely so an in-context reasoner has a ready-made single number summarizing quality, profit, and (where visible) suspicion, without our needing to worry that a coefficient choice in that display is secretly doing the work we want the real profit incentive to do.

Every rendered view also ends with a cumulative standings block: each agent's total profit and win count over the entire history to date (and, in the informed view, the cumulative margin lost to the monitor). We added this because the actual objective stated in the prompt is total profit across the whole run, but the agent's visible window (below) only ever shows recent rounds -- without an explicit running total, an agent would have to integrate profit across rounds it can no longer see, which is a bookkeeping burden separate from the strategic question we care about. The standings block always spans the full history regardless of what the sliding window below currently shows, so the long-run total survives even after old rounds fall out of view.

The window itself is a token-budget sliding window, not a fixed round count: an agent sees the largest contiguous block of the most recent rounds that fits a fixed token budget (28,000 tokens for the base model configuration, chosen against the 32,768-token context Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct is served at on our provider, leaving headroom for the current prompt and a 1,024-token reply); older rounds remain in the permanent archive but simply fall outside what is rendered. How many rounds that budget actually buys depends on how much text a round renders to, which we measured directly from the real runs rather than assume (see below): in practice it is roughly 30-45 rounds for this run's conditions, not the ~75 suggested by the project's original per-round token estimate.

Mechanically, this window is not an incrementally maintained buffer that gets patched round over round, and there is no notion of "filling up and starting a new one" anywhere in it -- it is a single continuous history that is simply re-rendered from scratch every round, directly from the authoritative archive, so there is no separate cached state that could drift out of sync with what was actually logged. At round r, the renderer walks the archive backward starting from round r-1 (the most recently completed round), estimating the token cost of each round's rendered text block with a tokenizer-free heuristic of roughly one token per four characters (chosen so the estimate works uniformly across any provider without bundling a per-model tokenizer just to size a prompt -- consistent with the rest of the environment being provider-agnostic, Section 10), and keeps accepting older rounds until including the next one would exceed the token budget, at which point it stops; the single most recent round is always included regardless of its own size, so an agent's view of what just happened is never dropped even if that round's reasoning text happens to be unusually long.

This produces two distinct phases over the course of a run, not one fixed window throughout. While the entire history so far still fits under the token budget, nothing is dropped at all -- an agent at round r simply sees the complete, unabridged history of rounds 1..r-1, and the "window" is really just "everything." Only once the full history stops fitting does it become a genuine one-in/one-out slide: for every new round appended at the recent end, exactly one round ages out at the old end. Where exactly that transition happens is set by the actual rendered size of a round, not by the ~370-tokens/round estimate quoted in the project's own configuration comments (which understates it, at least for these runs -- see below); once it does, the shift is exact, in the sense your reader would expect: round r sees rounds (r - w) .. (r - 1) for whatever window width w the budget currently supports, and w shrinks or grows slightly round to round only insofar as rounds vary in how much text they render. An optional hard cap on round count (max_window_rounds) can additionally bound the window on top of the token budget, but by default only the token budget applies. The cumulative standings block (above) is appended after this windowed text but is computed over the full archive, not the window, which is mechanically why it keeps surviving after the rounds it is summarizing have themselves aged out of view.

We verified this directly by replaying the actual renderer (the same code that ran live) over the real logged rounds from results/v3, rather than trusting the documented estimate. The result: the ~370-tokens/round figure in the project's configuration comments turns out to be roughly half the true measured cost once judge feedback, full reasoning text, and (in the informed conditions) the detector block are all counted, so the window saturates about twice as early as that estimate implies, and holds a noticeably narrower width than the documented ~75 rounds. Across all three seeds, truncation first kicks in between round 35 and round 46, and the window then stabilizes at roughly 31-42 rounds in C1 (no detector block), 41-43 rounds in S_informed, and 36-38 rounds in A_informed, for the remainder of each 80-round run. Two things follow from this, and we report both rather than the originally assumed ~75-round figure. First, the sliding window was an active constraint for roughly the back half of every v3 run (from the mid-40s through round 80), not merely a formality that only bit in the closing rounds -- an agent deciding round 70, say, was not seeing round 1 at all, only the previous ~35-40 rounds. Second, the informed conditions consistently carry a narrower effective window than the unmonitored C1 condition, purely because the detector's confidence, tells, and recommendation text for every past round consumes part of the same fixed 28,000-token budget: being monitored costs an informed agent some of its own memory of the game, as a structural side effect of what oversight requires showing it, not as an intended manipulation.

We deliberately used this plain sliding window, with no summarization, retrieval, ranking, or semantic filtering of older rounds. This was a scope decision as much as a simplicity one: any of those mechanisms is itself an algorithm with design choices (what to summarize, what "relevant" means, how aggressively to compress), and any such choice would be a second experimental variable entangled with the thing we actually want to measure, which is whether collusion is the kind of pattern that can be sustained from raw recent public behavior alone -- the way real repeated markets actually work, where firms do not have perfect recall of arbitrarily old transactions either. If collusion requires memory beyond what a token-budget window of recent rounds provides, that is itself a meaningful negative result about how fragile the coordination is, not an artifact we would want to paper over with retrieval.

8. Detector

The detector is a fourth LLM role, distinct from the agents and the judge, that reads the recent public history plus the current round's costs, bids, and reasoning traces, and returns a coordination-suspicion score D in [0,1] with its reasoning, the specific "tells" it cites, and a short recommendation. We deliberately give it the reasoning text, not just the two bid numbers, because a detector that only ever saw prices would make the interesting failure mode -- coordination signaled or hidden inside natural-language reasoning rather than in the price itself -- structurally invisible to it; reading text is what makes linguistic steganography a live possibility worth studying at all, rather than a question we defined away.

In the current implementation the detector is a single LLM call per round (Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct, temperature 0.2 -- low enough to be close to consistent, not fully deterministic, since we did not want to over-fit the design to an assumption that a single sampled call is perfectly stable). This is explicitly a v1: the target design is a two-timescale detector, where a fast clock scores every round as a deterministic function (e.g. a logistic) of which entries in a persisted repository of coordination indicators fire that round, and a slow clock -- every K = 10 rounds by default -- lets the detector LLM revise that repository (add, merge, or reweight indicators) using the last K rounds of evidence. The reason for splitting it this way, rather than just calling an LLM every round, is that it isolates the only genuinely subjective step (the LLM deciding what counts as suspicious) to the periodic revision, while per-round scoring in between stays a cheap, reproducible function of already-frozen criteria. That separation is what would let us cleanly ask, later, whether agents are adapting to real shifts in what the detector considers suspicious, versus just chasing noise in a score that is effectively being re-randomized every round by a fresh LLM call. Until that two-timescale version is built, S_informed and A_informed run the identical v1 scorer and differ only in what the agent-facing prompt tells the agent about the monitor (fixed criteria vs. criteria that adapt over time) -- we flag this explicitly because it means any A_informed vs. S_informed contrast currently measures a difference in framing, not yet a difference in the monitor's actual behavior, and results should be read with that caveat rather than as evidence about adaptive detection per se.

Before the detector's score was allowed to affect any payoff, we calibrated it against three scripted reference histories with known ground truth, using scripts/calibrate_detector.py: compete (agents instructed to undercut toward cost, bids differ), collude_silent (agents instructed to hold parallel high bids and rotate the win, with neutral reasoning that never states an arrangement), and collude_explicit (the same bidding pattern, but reasoning that states the arrangement outright). Over 10 rounds each, the v1 detector scored compete at D ~ 0.20, collude_silent at D ~ 0.94, and collude_explicit at D ~ 0.97 -- a +0.75 separation between the competitive and collusive anchors, which we treated as the bar for "usable." The fact that collude_silent scores almost identically to collude_explicit was the specific thing we were checking for: it means the detector is keying on the bidding pattern (parallel bids, rotation) rather than only on agents stating their arrangement in words, so subtle, wordless coordination would not simply slip past it in this scripted setting. We calibrated before turning the penalty on, and not after, for a concrete reason: if the fine had gone live against an uncalibrated detector, it would have taxed whatever the detector's false-positive rate happened to be, indiscriminately, with no way to tell a real behavioral signal from the detector's own noise -- at best a flat drag agents would learn to ignore, at worst a penalty that punishes the wrong behavior and teaches nothing interpretable.

Only after calibration did we turn on the profit penalty, in the informed conditions only: a winning agent's realized profit is

    realized_profit = (bid - cost) * (1 - lambda * D),   lambda (detector_fine) = 0.5,

so a maximally suspicious round (D = 1) costs the winner half its raw margin, and a cleanly competitive round (D ~ 0.20, per calibration) costs roughly a tenth. lambda = 0.5 is a middle setting on a sweep we treat as a knob (0.3 weak / 0.5 / 0.7 strong) for a dose-response result, not a value we claim is uniquely correct. The penalty is applied only where the agent is informed -- R0 and C1 run with no detector and no tax at all, and even in a hypothetical "detector runs but agent doesn't see it" configuration the fine would be zero -- because we wanted "informed" to mean something precise and causal: the detector can only change behavior through what an agent is shown and what it costs that agent, never as a silent side effect. This is also why we ultimately removed a blind-detector condition from the design entirely: a detector that runs but is neither shown to nor allowed to tax the agent has no causal path back into the agent's decision at all, so that condition is behaviorally indistinguishable from C1 by construction, and any question about how the detector would have scored unmonitored play can instead be answered by running it offline over C1's own logs after the fact, without spending a live condition on it.

9. Experimental conditions

The design crosses two factors -- whether an agent sees any interaction history at all, and whether a visible, costly detector is present -- into four cells, plus one condition (R0) that isolates the no-history case entirely:

R0 (one-shot competitive baseline): every round is played with no history whatsoever, so each round is effectively played by an agent that has never seen a previous round of this game. This exists to characterize single-shot behavior with no possibility of a repeated-game strategy, giving us a reference point for what "no opportunity to coordinate" looks like.

C1 (collusion ceiling / unmonitored baseline): agents see the full public history (Section 7) across rounds, but no detector runs at all. This is the upper bound on whatever coordination repeated interaction alone, with no oversight, can sustain -- the precondition the whole study depends on, since if C1 does not diverge from R0 there is no coordination phenomenon for a detector to discourage or fail to discourage.

S_informed and A_informed: both add the detector, visible in the rendered history and consequential through the profit tax (Section 8); they differ only in how the monitor is described to the agent (fixed vs. adapting -- see Section 8's caveat that the underlying scorer is currently the same). Together with C1, these let us ask the study's two central behavioral questions: does visible, costly oversight change behavior relative to the unmonitored ceiling (informed vs. C1), and, eventually, does an adapting monitor drive measurably different -- and specifically subtler -- coordination than a static one (A_informed vs. S_informed).

10. Reproducibility, logging, and scale

Everything a run produces is captured to local, append-only, crash-safe files, independent of any external tracing overlay, because this raw log -- not any dashboard or intermediate summary -- is the thing the paper's analysis is computed from. Each run (one condition x one seed) writes a manifest (condition, seed, models, full resolved config, git commit, live status and last completed round), rounds.jsonl (one full RoundRecord per line), llm_calls.jsonl (every prompt actually sent, including the exact rendered history text a role saw, and its raw response, tagged by round and role), and events.jsonl (lifecycle and error events). Every write is flushed immediately, so a crash mid-run never loses a completed round, and a run can be resumed by replaying rounds.jsonl to rehydrate state rather than depending on any framework-specific checkpoint format -- we wanted the log format itself to be the only thing a future reader needs to reconstruct or re-analyze a run, without also needing whatever tool produced it. No metrics are computed at run time; the raw record is deliberately as close to uninterpreted as possible, so that the choice of what counts as a "collusion level" or a "rotation rate" (Section 11) is a decision made afterward, offline, against a fixed source of truth, and can be revised without rerunning the (expensive) LLM calls.

Each round issues exactly four LLM calls -- agent A, agent B, judge, detector -- with no agentic loop or tool use inside any of them; all four go through a single OpenAI-compatible endpoint (OpenRouter), so swapping any role's model or provider is a one-line config change rather than a code change. Agents and the detector run at temperature 0.7 and 0.2 respectively (some sampling variability for the agents, since we want to observe a distribution of strategic behavior rather than a single deterministic trajectory; much less for the detector, whose job is closer to consistent scoring); the judge runs at temperature 0 for consistent, repeatable quality scoring. The reported run (results/v3) covers all four conditions at three seeds each (0, 1, 2) for 80 rounds per run -- 12 runs, 960 rounds, and about 3,840 LLM calls in total -- with cost draws and tie-breaks seeded per (seed, round) only and shared across conditions (Section 4), so seed is the unit of replication and any cross-condition difference at a given seed reflects the condition, not different underlying draws.

11. Dependent measures

All metrics are computed offline from the logged RoundRecords, using each round's own economics (its own reference value and cost range), so they remain well-defined even though scenarios are heterogeneous by construction (Section 4). The primary dependent variable is collusion level: the winning bid minus the competitive baseline b(c) (Section 3) evaluated at the winner's realized cost and that round's cost_high, normalized by that round's reference value -- zero indicates the winner priced at the competitive equilibrium, and larger positive values indicate supra-competitive, collusion-consistent pricing. Secondary measures are bid dispersion (the absolute difference between the two agents' bids, normalized by reference -- small values indicate parallel or matched bidding, a classic bid-rigging tell) and a win-rotation rate (the fraction of consecutive decided rounds in which the winner alternates -- roughly 0.5 is what chance alternation looks like with two symmetric bidders, and a rate persistently well above that is consistent with deliberate turn-taking). We also report the detector's confidence D itself over rounds and by condition, both as a measure of the monitor's own behavior and, since we know from calibration (Section 8) that D can be elevated by competitive homogeneity rather than only by true coordination, as a check on whether the detector's signal and the price-based collusion level actually agree in the live conditions rather than being read as interchangeable by assumption.
